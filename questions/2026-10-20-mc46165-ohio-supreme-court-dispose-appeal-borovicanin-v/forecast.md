## v1 — Initial forecast — 2026-10-20

### What resolves YES

The question resolves YES if, by 23:59 US Eastern Time on 30 October 2026, the Supreme Court of Ohio enters an order or opinion in Case No. 2026-1353 that ends the appeal. That covers: accepting jurisdiction *and* ruling; declining jurisdiction; dismissing the appeal (as moot, as improvidently allowed, or on the parties' voluntary dismissal or settlement); or affirming, reversing or vacating the First District's 2 October judgment (2026-Ohio-3885). It resolves NO if only procedural orders have been entered. Procedural orders include a ruling on the pending emergency stay motion (granting, extending, modifying or lifting the stay), a bare acceptance of jurisdiction followed by a briefing schedule, and orders setting argument. The sources are the court's docket and its daily case announcements. The question text calls it "the NCAA's appeal", but the appellants are actually the players. I read it as any final disposition of Case No. 2026-1353.

### Outside view

The Ohio Supreme Court normally takes months to decide whether to hear a discretionary (jurisdictional) appeal. In 2026 up to 11 June, the average from the appellant's memorandum to a jurisdictional decision was **74.4 days**; denials alone averaged 74.6 days, or 59.2 days when the appellee waived its response. The court accepted 6.55% of appeals [5]. The appellee's response is due 30 days after the memorandum (S.Ct.Prac.R. 7.03). Case 2026-1353 was filed in early October, so the NCAA's response is not due until around early November, after the deadline. On that routine timeline, a disposition within about four weeks of filing happens in well under 5% of cases. Some cases leave the routine track:
- **Voluntary dismissals.** The players' earlier appeal in this litigation (No. 2026-1245) was filed in mid-September and dismissed on the appellants' own application on 28 September, about two weeks later [2].
- **Sua sponte mootness dismissals.** The court has dismissed appeals as moot on its own motion in one-line entries, e.g. *Bowling v. DeWine*, 2022-Ohio-4122 [6].

Taking these into account, I put the outside view for this posture at about 10%.

### Inside view

**What the 14 October order says.** I read the order text from the official case announcement PDF [1]. The court did **not** stay the First District's judgment pending the whole appeal. The stay runs only "pending the submission and this court's consideration of the supplemental briefs". The briefs had to address "the effect of the governor's October 13, 2026 executive order on this action, including any potential mootness". They were due at 5 p.m. on 19 October, and the court barred any extensions. So a ruling is plainly expected soon, very likely before the 2 November season openers for Xavier and Cincinnati [3]. The question is what kind of ruling it will be:

| Next order | P(by 30 Oct) | Counts? |
|---|---|---|
| Rules on the emergency stay motion only (grants a stay pending appeal, or denies it), and the jurisdiction memoranda proceed on the normal schedule | ~65% | NO |
| Dismisses the appeal as moot because of Executive Order 2026-04D | ~9% | YES |
| Declines jurisdiction outright (which also ends the stay question) | ~5% | YES |
| Accepts jurisdiction and decides summarily (Rule 7.08(B)) | ~2% | YES |
| Players dismiss voluntarily, as in 2026-1245 | ~2% | YES |
| No order at all by 30 Oct (court still considering) | ~17% | NO |

**Why mootness is the main route to YES, and why it is still unlikely:**
- The court raised mootness itself, which gives it an off-ramp that avoids a merits ruling against the NCAA.
- But the executive order binds Ohio schools, not the NCAA, a private association. The NCAA has said that "schools that read the order differently bear the risk" [4], and it sued Governor DeWine in federal court on 16 October [4][7]. The players' dispute with the NCAA is therefore still live.
- Both sides probably have reasons to argue the case is not moot: the players need protection from NCAA sanctions, and the NCAA wants the First District's reversal to stand. That is my speculation, since the briefs have not been reported.
- A moot dismissal would also leave the players' eligibility hanging on an order now being challenged in federal court.

**Why declining jurisdiction is unlikely before 30 October.** The NCAA's memorandum in response is not due yet. The court usually waits for the response period to close, and declining now would amount to deciding the stay motion against the players.

**Overall.** Several factors push the estimate above the routine base rate: a court moving very fast, a self-raised mootness question, and a ban on extensions. But the most natural next order is a stay ruling, which counts as NO. I put the probability at about 18%.
